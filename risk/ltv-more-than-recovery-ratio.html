LTV is more than a recovery ratio

9 minfour controls hiding inside one ratio

Loan-to-value is usually discussed as a recovery metric. The standard logic is simple: if a borrower defaults, the lender should have enough collateral value to recover the principal outstanding after repossession and liquidation costs. That is certainly one role of LTV. But in asset-backed lending, LTV does much more than provide recovery cover.

A better way to think about LTV is as a combination of four controls: a behavioural control, a hedge against valuation error and fraud, a buffer against future asset-price uncertainty, and — finally — a recovery cushion.

1. LTV as a behavioural control

Borrower equity creates skin in the game. When a borrower contributes meaningful equity into an asset, walking away from the loan becomes more expensive. The borrower is not just defaulting on the lender’s money; they are also putting their own capital at risk. This can be particularly important during the early months of a loan.

Consider two borrowers purchasing the same ₹10 lakh vehicle.

  • Borrower A receives a ₹9.5 lakh loan and contributes ₹50,000.
  • Borrower B receives a ₹7.5 lakh loan and contributes ₹2.5 lakh.

The contractual obligation may otherwise look similar, but the economic incentives are very different. Borrower B has substantially more to lose if the asset is surrendered, repossessed or poorly maintained. The equity contribution therefore affects behaviour even before recovery becomes relevant.

In many portfolios, this relationship should be visible in early delinquency curves. The relevant question is not merely whether lower LTV produces better recovery after default, but whether it also reduces the probability of reaching default in the first place. In that sense, LTV can influence PD as well as LGD. That distinction is important.

2. LTV as protection against valuation error, fraud and manufactured equity

The second role of LTV arises from a basic asymmetry in secured lending: the loan amount is real, while collateral value is estimated. A lender disbursing ₹8 lakh has unquestionably advanced ₹8 lakh. But whether the underlying asset is actually worth ₹10 lakh is an estimate.

Valuation can be wrong for several reasons: appraisal error, inflated dealer invoices, poor asset inspection, adverse selection, related-party transactions, or outright fraud.

A meaningful equity contribution creates a natural hedge against these errors. Suppose an asset shown at ₹10 lakh is actually worth only ₹8.5 lakh. At 90% LTV, a lender disburses ₹9 lakh and is already underwater against the true asset value. At 70% LTV, the lender disburses ₹7 lakh — the valuation error is absorbed by the equity buffer rather than immediately becoming lender exposure.

There is another, less obvious benefit. Sometimes the borrower’s supposed equity is effectively financed by the dealer, anchor or another participant in the ecosystem. At first glance, this appears to defeat the purpose of LTV. If somebody else can fund the margin, perhaps the borrower has no real skin in the game. But sufficiently conservative LTV still creates friction.

Imagine again a ₹10 lakh asset. At 90% LTV, a dealer seeking to manufacture a transaction needs to arrange only ₹1 lakh of apparent equity. At 70% LTV, it needs ₹3 lakh. At 60% LTV, it needs ₹4 lakh.

Dealers and anchors generally operate with much smaller capital bases than lenders. Funding ₹1 lakh across ten artificial transactions requires ₹10 lakh. Funding ₹4 lakh across the same ten transactions requires ₹40 lakh. The economics of manufacturing transactions therefore deteriorate quickly as required equity increases. This gives LTV another important role: LTV does not necessarily eliminate fraud, but it can materially constrain the scalability of fraud.

That is especially relevant in dealer-led, anchor-led and embedded lending models where the lender does not independently control every part of asset origination and valuation.

3. LTV as a buffer against market and technology uncertainty

Even if today’s valuation is perfectly correct, tomorrow’s value is uncertain. LTV therefore also needs to compensate for future volatility in collateral prices. Commercial EV financing is a useful example.

An electric vehicle purchased today faces several sources of uncertainty: battery technology may improve, range may increase, battery costs may fall, charging infrastructure may improve, newer vehicles may have superior economics, OEMs may alter prices, government subsidies may change, and secondary-market depth may evolve.

Many of these developments are positive for the EV ecosystem but potentially negative for the residual value of an older-generation vehicle. A rapid technological upgrade could make a three-year-old vehicle economically less attractive even if it remains fully functional. There can also be positive shocks — a sharp increase in crude oil prices can improve the relative economics of electric vehicles and potentially support EV demand and resale values.

The point is not that EV prices will necessarily decline. The point is that the distribution of future values may be wider. And greater uncertainty should generally require a larger equity buffer. This suggests a useful distinction: LTV should depend not only on expected depreciation, but also on the uncertainty around depreciation. Two assets could have the same expected resale value after three years but deserve different LTVs if one has substantially higher residual-value volatility.

AssetExpected value after 3 yearsResidual-value uncertaintyAppropriate lending stance
Mature ICE vehicle₹5 lakhRelatively lowHigher LTV may be acceptable
New-generation EV₹5 lakhHigherLower LTV may be appropriate

The expected value is identical. The risk is not. This is why simply taking today’s invoice value and applying a standard haircut can be inadequate.

4. LTV as recovery protection

Only after these three roles do we reach the conventional interpretation of LTV: recovery. When a borrower defaults, the lender wants collateral proceeds to cover as much of the outstanding principal as possible. But even here, the relevant value is not simply the market value of the asset.

Recovery value is not market value

Recovery value = Market value − liquidation discount − repossession cost − refurbishment cost − legal and collection cost − value erosion during time to sale.

A vehicle theoretically worth ₹6 lakh may therefore produce only ₹5 lakh of net recovery. Meanwhile, principal outstanding at the time of default may still be ₹6 lakh or more. A prudent initial LTV creates enough headroom to absorb both contractual amortisation behaviour and collateral depreciation. This is the familiar LGD argument — but it is only one part of the story.

A broader way to think about LTV

The conventional credit framework tends to place LTV primarily inside LGD: lower LTV → higher collateral coverage → lower loss if default occurs. But in practice, the relationship is broader.

  • PD, because borrower equity changes incentives.
  • Fraud risk, because larger equity requirements increase the capital required to manufacture transactions.
  • Valuation risk, because equity absorbs errors in estimated collateral value.
  • Market risk, because equity provides a buffer against unexpected depreciation.
  • LGD, because collateral coverage improves recovery after default.

So rather than asking how much collateral do I have if the borrower defaults?, a better question is: how much uncertainty do I want the lender to absorb, and how much should be absorbed by the borrower or ecosystem participant?

That framing becomes particularly important in newer asset classes. For mature assets with deep secondary markets, stable technology and reliable valuation data, lenders can often operate at higher LTVs with reasonable confidence. For assets with uncertain residual values, thin resale markets, rapidly changing technology or significant dealer influence over valuation, the same LTV may create substantially greater risk.

This also means that a single LTV policy across asset classes can be misleading. The appropriate LTV should ideally reflect at least four things: borrower behaviour + valuation confidence + collateral volatility + recovery economics. Seen this way, LTV is not merely a recovery ratio. It is one of the most powerful structural risk controls available in asset-backed lending.
