Standard of living from an FI photo — an AI proof of concept

7 minSep 2026

Every field investigation ends with a judgment that no bureau score supplies: does the way this household lives match the income on the application, and the loan being asked for? A declared ₹80,000 a month sits awkwardly beside a single room with a tin roof; a modest declared income beside a fully furnished flat raises a different question. The check is critical — it is one of the few defences against inflated income — but it is also the most subjective call in the file.

That subjectivity is the problem. Two FI officers will grade the same house differently. There is no reference point to argue from, so a negative call is easy for Sales to contest as one person’s opinion, and a positive one leaves nothing on record to review later. This proof of concept asks a narrow question: can a model turn “it doesn’t look like ₹X” into “it looks like these five homes, which earn Y”?

What the tool does

Upload a photo of a home — the exterior, the kitchen, the floor, a living room. The tool returns an estimated monthly income and a likely range, an income level (four levels, grouped into Low, Medium and High), the five reference households it most resembles with their photos and incomes, and a confidence reading. When nothing in the reference set looks much like the photo, it says so and asks for manual review instead of producing a confident-looking number.

How it was built — seven steps

  1. A reference set with known incomes. About 850 photos of 43 Indian households, each with a recorded monthly income, spread across four income levels from subsistence to affluent. Every photo is tagged by type: exterior, street, front door, living room, kitchen, floor, wall, roof, ceiling, bedroom, toilet, and assets such as fan or AC, TV, fridge, two-wheeler and car.
  2. Labels on the right scale. Income is modelled on a log scale, so “off by 2×” means the same thing at ₹5,000 as at ₹5 lakh. Households are grouped into four levels and three tiers — Low (levels 1–2), Medium (3), High (4).
  3. Photos into vectors. Each photo is passed through an image-embedding model (CLIP, ViT-B/32), which turns it into 512 numbers. Photos that look alike — bare earth floors, tiled floors, modular kitchens — end up close together. The vectors are stored in a vector index.
  4. Nearest-neighbour matching. A new photo is embedded the same way and compared with every reference photo of the same type by cosine similarity. Each household keeps only its best-matching photo, so no single home dominates, and the five closest households are kept.
  5. The estimate. A similarity-weighted median of those five households’ incomes. The spread of their incomes, and how close the best match is, set the confidence.
  6. Honest testing. Each household was hidden in turn and predicted only from the other 42 — leave-one-household-out. Splitting by photo instead would have let the model recognise a second shot of the same kitchen and look far better than it is.
  7. Guardrails and deployment. A “no close match” threshold, a photo-type override for when the type is misread, exact detection of photos already in the reference set, and a browser-only build so that an applicant’s photo never leaves the device.

The headline is the middle column. The model never confused a Low household with a High one — the error that matters most for catching inflated income. Its weak spot is the Medium/High boundary: a tiled, furnished urban home often reads as High even when the household is Medium, and estimates are pulled toward the middle at both extremes.

Three results

A makeshift dwelling — timber and tin, tarpaulin, open ground. Estimated Level 2 · Low, with four of the five closest households in the bottom two levels. This is the case the tool handles best: the visual signal at the bottom of the distribution is unmistakable, and 60% of the vote lands on one level.

A multi-storey residential block shot from above. Estimated Level 3 · Medium, but with a split vote and a “no close match” warning: nothing in the reference set was photographed from this angle. This is the guardrail doing its job — the tool is telling the officer not to lean on it.

A furnished flat — vitrified tiles, wooden furniture, false ceiling. Estimated Level 4 · High, with every vote on the top level. Confidence is still marked low because the closest reference homes are not very close; a direction, not a number to underwrite on.

Income is shown as monthly income per adult-equivalent in purchasing-power-adjusted US dollars, the unit of the reference set. Converting to a declared household income in rupees is a policy step, not a model step.

Why this helps the Sales–Credit conversation

  • From opinion to comparison. The output is not “I don’t think so” but “this home most resembles these five, which earn between X and Y.” Sales can contest the comparison, but it is a comparison, with the photos on the table.
  • Consistency across officers. The same photo gives the same answer on every desk, in every branch.
  • An audit trail. The matched homes, the similarity scores and the confidence are all recordable — useful when a file is reviewed after it goes bad.
  • It knows when it doesn’t know. The no-close-match warning routes unusual cases to a person instead of forcing a number.

The decision stays human. The tool narrows the argument; it does not settle it.

Limits of the proof of concept

  • Small reference set. 43 households, only 8 in the Low tier. Every conclusion above carries that caveat.
  • One photo is noisy. A household is better judged from several photos — exterior, kitchen, floor, living room — than from any single one.
  • Colour and camera angle leak in. A brightly painted roof or an overhead shot can move the estimate more than construction quality does.
  • A photo is a weak proxy for income. Renters versus owners, joint families, rural wealth held in land, and frugal households all break the link. This supports a judgment; it cannot replace one.

What a production version needs

  1. A lender’s own reference set — past FI photos with verified income or repayment outcomes, which would lift accuracy far more than a bigger model.
  2. Fixed photo slots in the FI app (exterior, kitchen, living room, floor), with the model checking each photo matches its slot, and one combined estimate per household.
  3. A readable attribute layer — roof material, floor type, wall finish, visible assets — so every estimate comes with reasons, not just neighbours.
  4. The final comparison against the declared income and the requested EMI, returning Consistent, Needs review or Inconsistent — with the evidence attached for Credit and Sales to see.
