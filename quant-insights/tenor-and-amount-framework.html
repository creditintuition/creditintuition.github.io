Tenor and amount in unsecured lending

Part I — Frameworkexpected loss & variance of loss16 minDraft, Jul 2026
Abstract

A reduction in EMI caused purely by tenor extension should not be treated as equivalent to an improvement in credit quality. Unless the reduction in repayment burden produces a sufficiently large reduction in conditional default hazard, extending tenor increases lifetime credit loss per rupee. Therefore, an EMI-based eligibility rule cannot by itself justify increasing loan amount.

There is a case for longer tenor on absolute profitability and risk-adjusted return grounds — but only if the incremental risk is priced. That is a repricing argument, not a free lunch, and it is the subject of Part II.

1  The question

Business teams commonly argue that increasing tenor makes EMIs more affordable, and that this can be used either to approve a loan that would otherwise fail an EMI-to-income or EMI-to-ABB test, or to increase the sanctioned amount.

The answer differs by asset class. This series covers four: unsecured loans; depreciating collateral (vehicle, equipment); trading collateral (LAS, gold); and stable collateral (home loans, LAP). The last two are managed through LTV and margin discipline rather than a fixed amortisation schedule, so the framework here does not transfer to them directly.

For unsecured loans the position argued here is that on expected loss and variance of loss alone, extending tenor creates no room to increase the amount for a borrower whose inherent riskiness is unchanged. There is a case for extending tenor on absolute profitability and risk-adjusted return grounds, but only if the incremental risk is priced. That is a repricing argument, not a free-lunch argument, and it is the subject of Part II.

One assumption should be stated at the outset because everything rests on it: the hazard rate λ is held fixed as tenor changes. The claim being made is not that instalment burden is irrelevant to default. It is that the reduction in default rate from tenor extension is nowhere near proportionate to the tenor increase, and Section 4 shows that proportionate reduction is exactly what would be required.

2  Framework

Three parameters determine lifetime loss at amount level.

2.1 Hazard rate

The hazard rate λ is the proportion of surviving accounts in an origination cohort that default per unit of time. The general understanding is that most defaults occur early and that the hazard curve bends down with time. For unsecured loans of tenor up to three or four years, this has generally not been found to be the case in practice; the hazard rate is typically close to flat over that window. What bends is the amount default curve, and the bending comes from amortisation rather than from any improvement in the hazard rate.

This is a practitioner observation rather than a published result, and it is the single empirical input on which the rest depends. A reader who believes the hazard curve declines materially over three years should treat the conclusions as conservative and re-derive accordingly.

A constant hazard rate means survival decays exponentially in t, which is the form used throughout.

2.2 Amortisation schedule

Amortisation is assumed linear across months on book, A(t) = A0(1 − t/T).

This is a simplification, but it errs in a specific direction. A true EMI schedule is convex: the outstanding balance sits above the straight line throughout the life of the loan, because early instalments are interest-heavy. Exposure at default is therefore higher under the true schedule, and the amount loss estimated under linear amortisation is lower than what would actually be incurred.

The size of that gap depends on the interest rate, and at unsecured pricing it is not small. Over a 36-month schedule, average outstanding balance is 52.9% of principal at 12% APR, 55.7% at 24% and 58.5% at 36%, against 50% on the straight line. Expected loss under linear amortisation is therefore understated by roughly 6%, 12% and 17% respectively. At 60 months and 36% APR the understatement reaches 28%.

That direction matters, and the magnitude reinforces it. Linear amortisation is favourable to the case for increasing the amount, and the more expensive the loan, the more favourable it is. If the case fails under linear amortisation, it fails by a wider margin under the true schedule.

2.3 Loss given default

L denotes loss given default as a fraction of exposure at default, so loss on a defaulted account at t is L A(t). For unsecured lending L is high but not one; treating it as one overstates loss and distorts the relationship between the mean and the variance, since L enters the first moment linearly and the second quadratically. L is deterministic here. Appendix A.5 relaxes this and shows recovery dispersion adds a second, non-trivial variance term.

3  Expected loss and variance

Loss is incurred when an account survives to time t and then defaults, weighted by the balance still outstanding at that moment. Integrating that over the life of the loan, and doing the same with the squared loss amount, gives the mean and the variance in closed form. Both depend on tenor and hazard only through their product, x = λT.

For small x the two results reduce to:

E[Loss] ≈ (L A0 λT / 2) × (1 − λT/3)
Var[Loss] ≈ ((L A0)2 λT / 3) × (1 − λT)

The two approximations do not hold over the same range. For the mean, error stays under 1% to x = 0.30. For the variance it reaches 5.9% at x = 0.30 and 14.6% at x = 0.45, and the expression turns negative above x = 1. Since λ = 10% at a three-to-four-year tenor already puts x between 0.30 and 0.40, which is ordinary for medium-risk unsecured lending, the exact form should be used for variance in any portfolio or capital calculation. The small-x form is for intuition only.

Integrals, exact forms, derivations and accuracy in Appendix A.

3.1 Reading the expected loss

The leading term is L × (A0/2) × λT: loss given default, multiplied by average outstanding balance over the life of the loan, multiplied by the lifetime unit default rate. This is precisely the back-of-envelope calculation used in practice. The two factors then separate cleanly. The division by two is amortisation, present even if no account ever defaults early, and arising entirely because the balance at risk declines. The (1 − λT/3) term is survival, correcting for the fact that accounts defaulting early are not around to default later.

At x = 0.15, amortisation reduces loss by 50% and survival by a further 5% — roughly ten to one. This is the formal statement of the point in 2.1: when a cumulative amount curve flattens toward the end of a three-year product, essentially all of that flattening is amortisation. Very little principal remains outstanding to lose. It is not evidence that the surviving cohort has improved.

3.2 Unit and amount curves

The same integral, evaluated to an arbitrary time t rather than to maturity, gives the two curves that appear on every vintage chart: cumulative unit default and cumulative amount default. Both are stated in Appendix A.6.

At maturity D(T) equals the same function that governs expected loss, so the vintage curve and the loss calculation are one object viewed two ways. Figure 2 makes the mechanism explicit: the unit curve does not bend, the amortisation line falls, and their product is the amount curve.

4  The equivalence condition

Both results depend on tenor only through the product x = λT. Loss per rupee lent and loss volatility per rupee are functions of x alone; neither depends on A0. That single fact carries the argument.

4.1 What holding risk constant requires

If tenor is extended and the loss rate per rupee is to stay where it was, x must be unchanged, which requires λ to fall in exact inverse proportion to T.

Tenor changeRequired fall in λ
2y → 3y (+50%)33.3%
3y → 4y (+33%)25.0%
3y → 5y (+67%)40.0%
2y → 4y (+100%)50.0%

These are large numbers. A third of the hazard rate has to disappear to move a two-year product to three years. It is hard to accept that a reduction of that size follows from the instalment change alone, and it is not observed at anything approaching that magnitude.

If λ falls by less than proportionately, which is the realistic case, then x rises and the loss rate per rupee rises with it. At an unchanged hazard rate of 6.2% per year, loss per rupee runs 5.9% at two years, 8.7% at three, 11.4% at four and 14.0% at five. On that path the amount would have to be reduced, not increased, simply to hold total loss where it was.

4.2 The proportionate case still does not justify a larger amount

Suppose the claim is granted in full and λ does fall in exact inverse proportion to T. Then x is unchanged and so is the loss rate per rupee. But constant is not lower.

Expected loss is linear in A0, so holding the rate constant means total cohort loss scales exactly with the amount lent: a 20% larger loan produces 20% more expected loss. Variance is quadratic in A0, so the same increase produces 44% more variance. Nothing about the tenor extension has created headroom; at best it has left the borrower exactly where they were per rupee of exposure.

Both branches therefore lead to the same place. There is no version of the argument in which tenor extension, on its own, creates room for a larger amount.

4.3 What the affordability test was measuring

The EMI-to-income and EMI-to-ABB tests do become easier to pass when tenor is extended. The instalment genuinely falls. But those tests are not ends in themselves; they are proxies for default risk. If the instalment falls and the hazard rate does not follow it down proportionately, the proxy has been satisfied while the quantity it was designed to control has not improved. The rule has been met in form, and the risk it exists to limit is unchanged or worse.

That is the substance of the objection: the affordability test cannot itself be evidence that risk has fallen, because the link between instalment burden and hazard rate is exactly what is in dispute.

5  Conclusion

On the two measures developed here — expected loss and variance of loss, both at amount level — there is no case for increasing the loan amount when tenor is extended, provided the borrower’s inherent riskiness is unchanged.

That qualification should be stated precisely. Inherent riskiness is what underwriting assesses: income stability, business quality, repayment record, leverage held elsewhere. If it has genuinely improved, a larger amount may well be justified — but that is a re-underwriting decision, and it would justify the larger amount at the original tenor too. Tenor plays no part in it.

What is being rejected is the separate claim that the reduced instalment itself lowers risk enough to create room. The two branches in Section 4 close off both routes. If the hazard rate does not fall in inverse proportion to tenor, loss per rupee rises and the amount has to come down. If it does fall proportionately, loss per rupee is merely unchanged — and since expected loss is linear in A0 and variance quadratic, a larger amount raises both regardless.

The two channels should therefore not be conflated. A change in inherent riskiness is a reason to revisit the amount. A change in instalment burden, at best, leaves the risk position exactly where it was.

Finally, this is confined to the loss distribution. It says nothing about whether extending tenor is profitable, or whether it earns an adequate return on the capital it consumes. Those are separate questions, and they are where the defensible case for longer tenor lies. Part II takes them up: it introduces three definitions of equivalence between tenors — equal expected loss, equal profit per unit time, equal return on capital — and shows they imply materially different required reductions in hazard.

Appendix A  Derivations

Throughout, A(t) = A0(1 − t/T), the density of default at t is λe−λt, and x = λT. Loss on default at t is L×A(t).

Assumptions carried forward into Part II and not yet relaxed: no discounting, no prepayment, no fee income, no operating cost, no correlation structure, and λ held independent of instalment burden.

A.1 Expected loss

Loss is incurred when an account survives to t and then defaults, weighted by the balance outstanding at that moment:

           T
E[Loss] =  ∫  L × A₀(1 − t/T) × e^(−λt) × λ dt
          0

The λ dt factor is the default density. Without it the integral returns exposure-years rather than a rupee loss; this is a common slip.

Substitute u = λt, so dt = du/λ and the upper limit becomes x. The λ cancels:

                   x
E[Loss] = L A₀ ∫  (1 − u/x) e^(−u) du
                  0

Using the two standard integrals below:

A.2 Second moment

Same construction, squaring the loss amount:

              T
E[Loss²] =  ∫  (L × A₀)² (1 − t/T)² × e^(−λt) × λ dt
             0

Substituting u = λt as before:

                      x
E[Loss²] = (L A₀)² ∫  (1 − u/x)² e^(−u) du
                     0

Expanding the square gives three standard integrals, the last two by parts:

(1 − u/x)² = 1 − 2u/x + u²/x²

 x
 ∫ u² e^(−u) du = 2 − (x² + 2x + 2) e^(−x)
0

Collecting terms:

I₂(x) = (1 − e^(−x))
        − (2/x) [1 − (1 + x) e^(−x)]
        + (1/x²) [2 − (x² + 2x + 2) e^(−x)]

Multiplying through by x2 and gathering the e−x coefficients:

x² I₂ = x² − 2x + 2
        + e^(−x) [ −x² + 2x + 2x² − x² − 2x − 2 ]

      = x² − 2x + 2 − 2e^(−x)

The bracketed coefficient collapses to a constant, which is why the result is as compact as it is.

A.3 Variance

Squaring the mean:

h(x)² = (x − 1 + e^(−x))² / x²

      = [ x² − 2x + 1
          + 2(x − 1) e^(−x)
          + e^(−2x) ] / x²

Subtracting from I2(x), the x2 − 2x terms cancel and the constants leave 1:

x² [ I₂ − h² ] = (x² − 2x + 2 − 2e^(−x))
                − (x² − 2x + 1)
                − 2(x − 1) e^(−x)
                − e^(−2x)

            = 1 − e^(−x)[ 2 + 2x − 2 ] − e^(−2x)

            = 1 − 2x e^(−x) − e^(−2x)

so that:

Var[Loss] = (L × A0)2 × (1 − 2x e−x − e−2x) / x2

A.4 Small-x expansion of the variance

Expanding both exponentials to fourth order:

2x e^(−x)  = 2x − 2x² + x³ − x⁴/3 + …

e^(−2x)    = 1 − 2x + 2x² − 4x³/3 + 2x⁴/3 − …

numerator = x³/3 − x⁴/3 + …

The terms in x and x2 cancel exactly, so the numerator vanishes to third order at the origin. This is why the leading behaviour of the variance is x/3 rather than x. Dividing by x2:

Var[Loss] ≈ ((L A0)2 × x / 3) × (1 − x)

A.5 Random loss given default

With L random of mean L̄ and variance σl2, independent of default timing, condition on the default time and apply the law of total variance. The mean uses only E[L] = L̄, so E[Loss] = L̄ A0 h(x) unchanged. For the second moment, E[L2] = L̄2 + σl2, so:

E[Loss²] = (L̄² + σₗ²) A₀² I₂(x)

Var = (L̄² + σₗ²) A₀² I₂ − L̄² A₀² h²

    = L̄² A₀² (I₂ − h²)  +  σₗ² A₀² I₂

which is the timing-and-occurrence term plus the severity term, giving the decomposition into timing-and-occurrence variance and severity variance.

A.6 Summary of closed forms

Notation: A0 disbursed amount, T tenor, λ hazard rate per unit time, L loss given default, x = λT.

h(x) = (x − 1 + e^(−x)) / x
s(x) = √(1 − 2x e^(−x) − e^(−2x)) / x

E[Loss]    = L A₀ h(x)
sd[Loss]   = L A₀ s(x)

E[Loss²]  = (L A₀)² (x² − 2x + 2 − 2e^(−x)) / x²
Var[Loss]  = (L A₀)² (1 − 2x e^(−x) − e^(−2x)) / x²

Small-x forms, valid to approximately x = 0.3 for the mean and x = 0.2 for the variance:

E[Loss]    ≈ (L A₀ λT / 2) × (1 − λT/3)

Var[Loss]  ≈ ((L A₀)² λT / 3) × (1 − λT)

Cumulative curves:

Unit:      F(t) = 1 − e^(−λt)

                  − [1 − (1 + λt) e^(−λt)] / λT

A.7 Properties of the loss distribution

Standard deviation of loss, as a fraction of L A0, rises with tenor, peaks at x = 1.13 at a value of 0.360, and declines thereafter. The decline is not a risk improvement: at that point default is close to certain and the remaining uncertainty concerns timing rather than occurrence.

The coefficient of variation moves the other way. At x = 0.15 it is 2.91; at x = 0.45 it is 1.60. Relative dispersion falls as tenor rises even though absolute dispersion rises. Both statements are true and they are frequently confused; the absolute measure is the relevant one for capital, the relative measure is not.

A single-loan variance is not a capital number. Idiosyncratic variance diversifies at 1/√n and is not capitalisable. What is derived here is the building block; converting it into economic capital requires a correlation structure, which is deferred to a later part.
