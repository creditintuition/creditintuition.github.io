On RBI’s proposal to restrict revolving credit for NBFCs

8 minwhy prohibition is the wrong instrumentAug 2026

To understand RBI's apprehensions around NBFCs doing line-of-credit business, start with its apprehensions around NBFCs doing credit cards, then tie it back. Why credit cards? Because this is also a revolving product, and one whose mechanics are more widely understood. The two rationales are not identical — the credit card restriction is largely a payment-systems argument, while the current proposal is about product structure and asset classification — but the mechanics of a revolver are common to both.

Banks essentially do three things from one entity: savings and deposits, payments, and lending. RBI, through entities like payments banks and NBFCs, has been trying to widen the spread of these services while keeping risk contained. A payments bank cannot lend. An NBFC is largely built for lending, not for deposits and payments.

A credit card is a lending instrument and a payment instrument at once, which is why it belongs in the ambit of bank issuance. A payment-instrument failure — or a failure of the counterparty providing novation services — can propagate much further through the payment ecosystem than a lending failure alone, and because credit card money is borrowed money, that failure can be larger than a purely debit or prepaid-card failure. So I agree with restricting payment instruments from being used for credit — the fintech innovations around prepaid cards used for credit were shut down in June 2022 — and with banks holding credit card licences exclusively, though distribution through co-branded cards is allowed and it is worth being careful about who is doing the actual underwriting.

The current draft carries this entity-based logic forward: the restriction does not apply to NBFCs already authorised to issue credit cards, of which there are two — SBI Card and BoB Cards. Both are bank subsidiaries. The exemption turns on who the entity is, not on whether the product is safer in their hands.

Why a revolver is riskier than a term loan

Other than the payments question, a line-of-credit product — including credit cards — carries higher risk because it allows three things a term loan does not.

  • Evergreening. The borrower can keep paying interest without any principal reduction, and no default is reported. What makes this specific to a revolver is not that evergreening is possible — term loans get evergreened too, through top-ups and refinance — but that in a revolver it requires no new credit decision. It is automatic, and it leaves no approval trail.
  • Wrong-way risk. Once the line is issued, the option sits with the line holder, and full utilisation typically happens in states of distress. This is the same problem the Basel framework addresses through credit conversion factors on undrawn commitments.
  • Asset-liability mismatch. Since the option to draw lies with the borrower, ALM mismatch can spike — and NBFCs have neither sticky liabilities (deposits) nor a liquidity facility (RBI repo) to absorb it.

There is an additional issue today with line of credit specifically. Combined with UPI, it becomes functionally a credit card — the UPI mandate's novation features let the buyer pay at a later date. In practice, credit line on UPI was opened to banks and subsequently to small finance banks, and was never available to NBFCs. This is a question of where the rule goes next, not what it currently takes away.

The draft's actual scope

The proposal seems reasonable from a risk perspective. It is worth being clear that this is a draft — RBI issued the amendments to the Credit Facilities Directions on 6 August 2026, with comments open until 28 August — and that its scope is wider than line of credit. Revolving credit is defined residually: any fund-based facility that is not a term loan. That catches cash credit, overdrafts, flexi loans and working capital lines, and on the industry's own estimate affects a book of over ₹2 trillion.

Where this note starts from

I will take the supervisory concern as well-founded. The question is not whether the risk is real but whether prohibition is the instrument that reaches it. Line of credit is an important product, particularly for the MSME segment. And there are other revolver-like products that share some of the same drawbacks — gold-loan overdrafts, for instance, which fall squarely within the residual definition.

On liabilities

RBI is right on the on-demand-liability side of NBFCs versus sticky deposits. But this is a liquidity problem, and liquidity problems are solved with buffers, not prohibition — liquidity coverage and net stable funding requirements calibrated to include undrawn commitments. On the solvency side, capital allocation could be moved to the line issued rather than the utilisation, which is what a credit conversion factor already does for banks. The machinery exists; it has simply never been applied here. This does make the product more expensive, but there is a price for risk.

It is worth being precise about what pricing can and cannot do. Pricing works for the credit-risk residual, once mitigants are in place. It does not work for liquidity risk — a higher coupon on drawn balances does not produce cash in a stress, and there is no lender of last resort behind an NBFC. Buffers for liquidity, pricing for credit.

On who should be allowed to do it

The objection to all of this is that these requirements presuppose ALM and modelling capability that is not uniform across 9,000-plus NBFCs, and that a regulator writing one rule has to write for the weakest entity. That is fair, and it argues for eligibility conditions rather than prohibition. RBI already runs this model — SBR layering, deposit-taking permission, credit card authorisation with net-worth thresholds. The important design point is that eligibility should be rules-based and self-certifying — demonstrated liquidity coverage on undrawn commitments, CCF-based capital, disclosed exposure metrics — rather than a discretionary approval route. Where permission has been discretionary, as with credit card authorisation, the outcome has been indistinguishable from a ban.

On UPI and LOC linkage

This is a fair ask, and if it is delinked there is still a large segment that will take the product. NBFCs should be okay with restrictions on not allowing line of credit on UPI.

On evergreening

This is a risk-management issue, and it can be addressed through DPD classification. Tie DPD strictly to principal payment. It ties the borrower to principal reduction while retaining flexibility around drawdown. A version of this already exists in the IRAC framework — the “out of order” test for cash credit and overdraft accounts, where an account with no credits for 90 days, or credits insufficient to service interest, is classified non-performing. What is missing is a positive requirement for principal step-down. That is the refinement worth arguing for, rather than presenting it as a new idea.

On evaluating the risk

One of the risks the regulator is trying to solve for is risk evaluation itself. Through CASA accounts, banks have access to the borrower's transaction information and are better able to manage the risk. This can largely be solved via account aggregator, with higher fetch frequency and longer consent duration — both configurable today.

Two limits should be acknowledged. Consent is revocable as a matter of law and cannot be contracted away, and account linkage is selective — a borrower can shift flows to an unlinked account. Neither is fatal, but both have to be treated as monitoring signals rather than ignored: consent revocation, or a material drop in credit flows through linked accounts, should be a hard trigger that freezes further drawdown. My belief remains that borrowers will give consent if they are genuinely seeing the benefit from the product — but the design should not depend on that belief holding at the moment of deterioration.

On enforcement in spirit

What matters in a line of credit is at-will drawdown. The likely industry response to a term-loan-only rule is not exit but restructuring: an internal, uncommitted exposure limit on the customer, with each drawdown issued as a separate bullet loan, credit evaluation at drawdown run automatically against risk appetite, and KYC held at entity level with frequent updates. Nothing about this fails the draft's definition. Nothing is sanctioned to the borrower, so nothing is replenished, and the depth of the check at each drawdown is a matter for the NBFC's own credit policy.

The point is not that this is evasion. It is that the restriction displaces the risk into a form that is harder to supervise. A revolver at least shows a continuous, visible exposure. A chain of bullet loans shows a sequence of fully repaid, performing accounts, and the DPD test — including the principal-linked version proposed above — passes by construction, because the repayment of one loan is funded by the disbursement of the next. The only rule that reaches this today is the treatment of refinance as restructuring under the 2019 Prudential Framework, and that applies only where the borrower is in financial difficulty — a determination made under each lender's own board-approved policy, one that retail and small-ticket lending has never in practice applied.

The point

If the concern is that borrowers are being carried without principal reduction, the test that actually reaches both structures is net exposure over time rather than per-account status: cumulative disbursement against cumulative collection at borrower level, the gap between one maturity and the next disbursement, and whether the borrower ever reaches zero. Those are three numbers. They are already in every NBFC's systems, and none of them require anyone to opine on how thorough the underwriting was.

The proposal in its current form increases the cost of doing business without providing a full remedy. Requiring the disclosure and supervision of net principal reduction would.
