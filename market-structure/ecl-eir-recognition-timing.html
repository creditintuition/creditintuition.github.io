ECL and EIR — recognition timing and its implications

11 minthe case for parameter standardisation

This note separates three things: the mechanics of the two changes, which are established; their implications for reported financials, capital and pricing, which follow from the mechanics; and an assessment of whether the correction is calibrated correctly, which is opinion and is labelled as such in the final section.

1. Scope and background

Two changes to recognition timing have been introduced into Indian lending, roughly nine years apart.

Non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) adopted Ind AS 109 under the Ministry of Corporate Affairs roadmap of March 2016 — entities with net worth above ₹500 crore from FY2018-19, the remainder from FY2019-20. Banks were scheduled for the same period, deferred by RBI in April 2018 and again in March 2019. The stated obstacle was statutory: bank financial statement formats are prescribed in the Third Schedule to the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, and accommodating Ind AS presentation required amendment that did not follow.

RBI issued the Commercial Banks (Asset Classification, Provisioning and Income Recognition) Directions, 2026 on 27 April 2026, effective 1 April 2027. The Directions prescribe expected credit loss (ECL) provisioning and effective interest rate (EIR) income recognition directly under Sections 21 and 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, rather than by adopting Ind AS. Loans outstanding at 31 March 2027 must be brought under EIR by 31 March 2030, and the incremental provisioning impact may be spread over four years to 31 March 2031, with common equity tier 1 (CET1) capital add-backs during the transition. Small Finance Banks, Payments Banks and Local Area Banks are outside scope.

The first change concerns income recognition. A processing fee was previously recognised at disbursal. Under the effective interest rate method it is treated as part of the instrument’s yield.

The fee, restated

A loan of ₹100 against which ₹3 is collected upfront represents ₹97 of cash disbursed, and is recognised at ₹97. The contractual repayments are unchanged, so discounting them to a lower carrying amount produces a higher effective yield. The fee is reclassified from fee income to interest income and released over the life of the exposure.

The second concerns loss recognition. Under the Income Recognition, Asset Classification and Provisioning (IRAC) framework, provisioning followed identification of impairment, conventionally at 90 days past due. Under expected credit loss, provision is recognised at origination. Exposures are classified in three stages — performing, significantly deteriorated since origination, and credit-impaired — with twelve-month expected loss in Stage 1 and lifetime expected loss from Stage 2. The 2026 Directions impose minimum provisions irrespective of model output: 0.40% in Stage 1 and 5.00% in Stage 2 for standard corporate and retail exposures.

Both changes address the same distortion in opposite directions. IRAC recognised losses after the fact; upfront fee recognition recognised income before it was earned. Both overstated the reported position of a lender originating heavily in the current period.

2. Effective interest rate: mechanics and limitations

Over the full life of an exposure, EIR has no effect. The cash received from the borrower is unchanged, and total income recognised is identical under both methods. Only the period of recognition differs, with a portion of first-year income displaced into later years.

On a portfolio that is not growing, the period effect also disappears. Fees deferred on new originations are offset by fees released from seasoned exposures. The unamortised balance stabilises and the annual effect is nil.

EIR is therefore a measurement change rather than a charge. Income is recognised as the exposure earns it.

Effect on growing portfolios. Where originations exceed run-off, deferrals exceed releases in every period and the offset does not arise. A lender in build phase, or one whose entire book is young, reports lower income than it would have until growth flattens. Underlying profitability is unchanged; the reported figure is displaced.

Two limitations on the “no net effect” proposition. The proposition holds only where the exposure runs to contractual maturity. Prepayment terminates the exposure early and the unamortised balance is recognised in that period. Default terminates it without the fee being earned.

More significantly, EIR requires an estimate of expected rather than contractual life, which imports an assumption about prepayment behaviour. Ind AS 109 requires revisions to estimated cash flows to be reflected by restating the gross carrying amount, with the adjustment recognised in profit or loss. In Indian retail lending, where prepayment rates move with rate cycles and balance-transfer activity, this is a live source of earnings variation.

This qualifies a common characterisation. EIR is not purely arithmetic — it contains a behavioural estimate, and that estimate generates volatility of its own. The distinction from ECL is one of degree of model dependence, not its presence or absence.

3. Expected credit loss: origin and rationale

The condition ECL was designed to address is documented and specific.

Under IRAC, loss recognition lagged origination by two to three years in most retail and MSME portfolios. A lender growing quickly recognised full income across the growth period while the associated defaults surfaced later. Reported profitability in the early years was systematically overstated relative to the eventual outcome.

The consequence was not confined to measurement. It created an incentive structure in which growth was rewarded before its credit outcome was observable. The Indian unsecured lending cycle of 2022 to 2024 is consistent with this pattern, though attribution to accounting treatment alone would be an overstatement — distribution economics, bureau saturation and funding availability were also operative.

ECL removes the lag by requiring provision at origination on the basis of expected loss.

4. Expected credit loss: the absence of an amortising principle

The design question is not whether losses should be recognised early. It is whether recognising twelve months of expected loss at origination, with no corresponding spreading mechanism, is proportionate.

EIR spreads the fee across the life of the exposure, tracking the amortising balance. ECL recognises the twelve-month loss immediately, for an exposure whose losses may emerge across a longer horizon. There is no equivalent recognition profile.

The standard already contains the alternative. For purchased or originated credit-impaired financial assets, Ind AS 109 applies a credit-adjusted effective interest rate, in which expected credit losses are incorporated into the estimated cash flows used to determine the rate. Expected loss is then recognised across the asset’s life through the yield. The mechanism exists; it is confined to one category of asset.

It was also once proposed generally. The International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) Exposure Draft ED/2009/12, Financial Instruments: Amortised Cost and Impairment, published in November 2009, proposed integrating expected credit losses into the effective interest rate for all financial assets measured at amortised cost. It was not adopted. The reasons recorded were operational — the loss-adjusted rate had to be determined at initial recognition, and revisions to expected loss required catch-up adjustments — and the post-crisis policy preference was for earlier recognition rather than better matching.

The proposition here is therefore narrower than a general objection to conservatism: the amortising principle is available within the standard, was proposed for general application, and was set aside for reasons that were practical rather than conceptual.

5. Comparative implications for AUM, equity and capital

The two changes are frequently discussed together. Their implications for assets under management (AUM), equity and capital differ.

The final row is the substantive distinction. On a flow basis the two are similar — both neutral in steady state, both negative during growth. On a stock basis they differ: EIR defers income, ECL absorbs capital.

Two consequences of the transition treatment merit attention.

First, the absence of a P&L effect does not limit the capital effect. Retained earnings form part of CET1 capital, and reserves constrain dividend capacity.

Second, because the transition adjustment is never recognised in any period’s income statement, it does not appear in any reported return on assets (ROA) or return on equity (ROE) series. Returns before and after transition are not directly comparable, and the discontinuity is not flagged in the primary statements. Restating comparatives would address this; it is not standard practice.

Not all of this is presentational. The two changes differ most in whether the effect stops at the reported number.

EIR’s effect is largely presentational. Lifetime income is unchanged, the deferred balance is recoverable, and no cash moves. What changes is the period in which income appears.

ECL’s effect is economic. The capital absorbed is not recovered unless the losses fail to materialise. Reduced CET1 reduces the volume a lender can carry at a given leverage, so provisioning at origination raises the capital cost of growth itself — a decision variable rather than a reporting outcome. A lender that must provision on day one has also to price for that provision, which alters the rate required on new business, most sharply in segments where expected loss is large relative to margin. Where floors bind, the required rate is set by the floor rather than by the lender’s own loss estimate, which compresses the risk-based pricing gradient at the lower-quality end.

The transition adjustment sits between the two. It bypasses the income statement, but it consumes CET1, constrains dividend capacity and reduces lending capacity from the date of recognition.

6. Implications for growing portfolios

The neutrality results in Sections 2 and 5 hold in steady state. Few Indian lenders are in steady state.

Where originations exceed run-off, neither change is offset. Deferrals exceed releases and new provisions exceed releases from run-off in each period until growth flattens. The two effects are additive, and the ECL component is the larger of the two, because provisions scale with the full exposure while deferred fees scale only with the fee.

A secondary consequence has received little attention: the direction of reporting bias has reversed. Under IRAC, growth improved reported earnings and contraction exposed accumulated losses. Under ECL and EIR the reverse applies — growth depresses reported earnings, while a contracting book releases both deferred fee and provision stock into the income statement, improving reported returns as the portfolio shrinks.

This is a change in the sign of the cyclical reporting bias. It does not appear to have been incorporated into how Indian lenders or analysts read reported returns across the cycle.

Whether it constitutes a defect is arguable. A lender growing rapidly is originating exposure faster than outcomes can be observed, and provisioning at origination reinstates a discipline that delayed recognition had removed. The effect is nonetheless real and warrants explicit adjustment when comparing lenders at different points in their growth cycle.

7. Model dependence and parameter variation

A separate issue, and over a longer horizon possibly a more consequential one, is the variability of ECL outputs.

Two lenders holding materially similar portfolios can report materially different provisions through differing treatment of the probability of default (PD) term structure, the significant increase in credit risk (SICR) trigger, the forward-looking macroeconomic overlay, and — for revolving exposures — the exposure period, which Ind AS 109 permits to extend beyond the contractual period where the lender’s exposure is not limited to contractual notice. There is no natural anchor for the latter.

There is precedent for how supervisors respond to this. The Basel Committee withdrew the Advanced Measurement Approach for operational risk in the December 2017 finalisation of Basel III, citing the complexity of the approach and the lack of comparability arising from a wide range of internal modelling practices. The advanced internal ratings-based (AIRB) approach was restricted for several exposure classes in the same package, and an aggregate output floor of 72.5% of the standardised result was introduced. The direction of regulatory travel over the preceding decade was away from internal models.

The AMA precedent has a limit, and it should be stated. AMA and IRB are capital frameworks, administered by supervisors, with comparability and prudence as objectives. ECL is an accounting requirement, administered by standard setters, with faithful representation as its objective. Expected loss is entity-specific by construction. There is no equivalent withdrawal mechanism.

The constraint is therefore applied through floors rather than through replacement of the approach. RBI’s Stage 1 and Stage 2 minima are a form of standardisation, imposed as a binding minimum.

The risk in floor-based standardisation

Where the floor binds for a substantial proportion of exposures, model output ceases to determine the reported provision. The result is the cost of a modelled framework without the discriminating benefit of one. Whether the floors bind in practice is an empirical question that current disclosure does not resolve; portfolio-level Stage 2 data would settle it.

An alternative design would publish reference parameters by product and ticket size, calibrated on system-wide data, against which lenders model and disclose deviation. Comparability is obtained through the disclosed deviation rather than through a binding minimum, and the deviation becomes the object of audit and supervisory review. Bank of Spain’s Annex IX operates on a comparable principle, providing supervisor-calibrated provisioning parameters as an alternative to internal estimates.

8. Assessment

This section is opinion. The preceding sections are not.

EIR is directionally correct. It recognises revenue as it is earned. Its costs are borne disproportionately by growing and early-stage portfolios, and the prepayment and default cases qualify the claim that it is economically neutral over the life of an exposure. On balance the trade is reasonable.

ECL addresses a real defect and appears overcorrected in execution. The regime it replaced permitted income to be recognised ahead of the credit outcome. Replacing back-loaded recognition with fully front-loaded recognition, absent any amortising mechanism, is a prudential judgement rather than a measurement one — particularly given that the standard applies precisely that mechanism to credit-impaired assets and declines to extend it.

Parameter variability remains unresolved. Without standardisation by product and ticket size, ECL produces either provisions that are not comparable across lenders or provisions determined by floors, in which case the modelling apparatus is redundant. The current trajectory is toward the second outcome, and it does not appear to have been selected deliberately.
