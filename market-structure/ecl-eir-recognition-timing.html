ECL and EIR

12 minwhat changed, what it does to the books, where it has overcorrectedAug 2026

1  The two changes, and why they landed nine years apart

Two things changed in how Indian lenders report their loan books. They are usually discussed separately. Together they do something neither does alone.

EIR changed when income is recognised. Take a loan of ₹100 with a 3% processing fee. Earlier, the ₹3 was booked as income on the day of disbursal. Under the effective interest rate method it cannot be. The lender has actually parted with ₹97 of cash, so the loan goes onto the balance sheet at ₹97, not ₹100. The borrower still repays on the full ₹100 at the contractual rate. The same repayments now discount back to a smaller carrying amount, which means the implied yield is higher than the rate on the sanction letter. The fee has not disappeared — it has been reclassified from fee income to interest income and released over the life of the loan.

So the mechanics are: fee netted off, smaller asset, higher yield. The causality runs in that order. The yield is the output, not the cause.

ECL changed when losses are recognised. Under IRAC, provisioning followed the event. A loan was provided against once it had gone bad, conventionally at 90 days past due. Under expected credit loss, the lender provides at origination. Loans sit in three stages — performing, significantly deteriorated since origination, and credit-impaired. Stage 1 carries a provision for losses expected over the next twelve months. From Stage 2 it steps up to lifetime expected loss. RBI has also set minimum provisions irrespective of what a lender's model produces: 0.40% in Stage 1 and 5.00% in Stage 2 for standard corporate and retail exposures.

On the sequencing

NBFCs went first, by roughly nine years — Ind AS 109 from FY2018-19 for the larger ones, FY2019-20 for the rest. Banks are only now coming in, under RBI's Directions of April 2026, effective April 2027.

This was not a judgement about who was ready. NBFCs are companies, so MCA's Ind AS roadmap swept them in automatically. Banks report in a format fixed by the Third Schedule of the Banking Regulation Act, which Ind AS presentation does not fit, and the amendment never came — which is why the 2026 Directions prescribe ECL and EIR directly under Sections 21 and 35A rather than adopting Ind AS at all. It also helped that 2018-19 was the trough of the asset quality review, when public sector banks were in no position to absorb a day-one equity hit.

The result is that the entities with the thinnest data, the least model governance and the smallest audit budgets went first. That was an accident of company law.

2  Both changes correct the same distortion

Under IRAC, losses were recognised late. Under upfront fee recognition, income was recognised early. Both flattered the lender writing a lot of new business right now.

So the direction is coherent: income was being pulled forward, losses were being pushed back, and both are now being forced toward the period they economically belong to.

But the two are not the same kind of thing, and this is where most of the confusion sits.

One is arithmetic, the other is policy

EIR is matching. It creates no buffer, changes no economics, and leaves lifetime income exactly as it was. It is a measurement correction.

ECL with regulatory floors is conservatism. It recognises a loss before one has occurred, and the floors deliberately provide more than the expected loss. It is a prudential buffer.

But because the entire fee on the back-book was earlier charged upfront, reported profit and revenue will be lower than the year before — not just in the growth years, but for as long as it takes the deferred-fee balance to season to its steady-state level.

Where the logic holds

Over the life of the loan EIR makes no difference at all. The lender collects the same rupees from the same borrower and total income is identical. Only the period of recognition changes.

On a book that is not growing, this converges to nil — but only in steady state, once the unamortised balance has seasoned to its equilibrium level, which takes roughly one average loan tenor. In the interim, including the year of transition itself, deferrals on new originations are not yet matched by releases from an equivalent back-book, because the back-book was never deferred in the first place — it was reported in full under the old method. So even a flat book takes a genuine, multi-year step down in reported income relative to the year before, before the effect actually nets to nil.

This is not a bad outcome. It allows revenue to be recognised when it is truly earned.

Where the logic breaks

All of the above assumes the loan runs to term. Two things stop it. A borrower who prepays extinguishes the loan early, and the whole unamortised balance is recognised in that period. A borrower who defaults extinguishes it too, and the fee is never earned at all.

More awkwardly, EIR requires an estimate of expected life, not contractual life, which means an assumption about prepayment behaviour. When actual behaviour diverges — and in Indian retail lending prepayment moves sharply with rate cycles and balance-transfer competition — the carrying amount is restated and the difference runs through P&L as a catch-up.

So EIR is not purely arithmetic either. It carries a behavioural estimate inside it. The difference from ECL is one of degree of model dependence, not its presence or absence.

4  Why ECL was introduced

The problem it was built to solve was real.

Under IRAC, NPAs were recorded on a back-ended basis. A lender could grow aggressively, book full income across the growth years, and let the defaults surface two or three years later — by which point the track record was already established and, usually, already rewarded.

It was easy to book profits in the early years while the defaults built up at a later stage. That is a misaligned incentive, not just a measurement problem. Anyone who watched Indian unsecured lending between 2022 and 2024 has seen what it looks like in practice.

ECL removes the lag. Provide for what you expect to lose from the day you lend.

5  But ECL is the other extreme

The objection is not to recognising losses early. It is that ECL corrects a back-ended regime by front-loading everything, with no amortising principle attached to it.

EIR spreads the fee across the life of the loan, tracking the balance as it amortises. Income arrives as the exposure earns it. ECL does the reverse — it recognises twelve months of expected loss immediately, at origination, for a loan whose losses may in fact emerge across three years. There is no equivalent spreading over the period in which the loss actually occurs.

What I would want instead

If expected loss is a known cost of the yield, it should be recognised on the same profile as the yield — amortised along the loss-emergence path. That is true recognition, consistent with what EIR already does for the fee.

What I would not want, and this distinction matters: I am not asking for provisions to build up gradually as vintages season. That is just NPA recognition in different clothing — recognition would lag emergence again, which is the exact failure ECL was written to fix. The point is to amortise a loss that is known at origination, not to defer recognising it until it shows up.

The standard already contains the mechanism. For purchased or originated credit-impaired assets, Ind AS 109 applies a credit-adjusted EIR, where expected loss is embedded in the yield and recognised across the asset's life — exactly as the fee is. The logic is conceded and then confined to one narrow category. The arbitrary thing is the ring-fence, not the principle.

And it was once proposed generally. The IASB's exposure draft of November 2009 proposed integrating expected losses into the effective interest rate for all financial assets at amortised cost. It was abandoned on operational grounds — the loss-adjusted rate had to be fixed at origination and every revision generated catch-up adjustments — and because the G20, after the financial crisis, wanted losses recognised earlier rather than better matched. It was not abandoned because the principle was wrong.

All of it helps risk management. But it is more punitive than it needs to be.

6  Stable books are fine. Growing books are not.

In a stable state ECL reaches the same effect — provisions approximate actual credit losses, and the charge in each period is roughly the loss in that period. The same holds for EIR: new deferrals offset old releases and the annual effect is nil. So on a stable book none of these mechanics matter much.

A growing book has no such offset. Originations exceed run-off, so deferrals exceed releases and new provisions exceed releases from run-off, every single period, until growth flattens. The two effects stack. ECL is the larger of the two, because provisions scale with the whole exposure while deferred fees scale only with the fee.

The lender is not less profitable than before. It is pre-funding the profit of its future steady state. But reported earnings are worse, and analysts do not adjust for it.

The reporting bias has inverted

Under IRAC, growth flattered earnings and contraction exposed the losses. Under ECL and EIR the opposite holds — growth is penalised in reported earnings, and a shrinking book releases deferred fee and provision stock into P&L, so returns improve as the franchise contracts. That is a change in the sign of the bias across the credit cycle.

7  The model dependence problem

Separate from timing, and over a longer horizon possibly more consequential. ECL is model dependent, and the estimates can vary a great deal with the assumptions and the model. Three specific problems.

Manipulation runs both ways

IRAC discretion was one-way — under-provide, evergreen, restructure. ECL discretion runs in both directions, which allows earnings smoothing either way. That is a harder supervisory problem, not an easier one.

Subjectivity begins at Stage 2

The Stage 1 twelve-month PD is actually the most estimable parameter available — vintage curves observe it directly. It is the SICR trigger and the lifetime PD that are subjective, because lifetime PD needs a term structure and a macro overlay extrapolated well past any observable horizon.

EAD, and revolving exposures

EAD is hard to determine and particularly cumbersome for a line of credit. Ind AS 109 permits the exposure period on a revolving facility to run beyond the contractual period. That means behavioural EAD with no natural anchor. Two lenders with identical books can produce materially different numbers.

The supervisory precedent, and its limit

We have seen where this leads. Basel rolled back the AMA — the advanced measurement approach for operational risk — in the December 2017 finalisation, because there were too many subjective calls being taken by banks and the numbers were not comparable across them. The advanced IRB approach was restricted at the same time and an output floor of 72.5% imposed. Fifteen years of regulatory direction has been a retreat from internal models. ECL arrives now, going the other way.

The AMA analogy has a limit worth stating. AMA and IRB are capital frameworks, owned by supervisors, where the objective is comparability. ECL is accounting, owned by the IASB, where the objective is faithful representation. Expected loss is entity-specific by construction, so there is no rollback mechanism. IASB will not revert IFRS 9 and RBI cannot.

So the retreat comes through floors instead — and that is the risk in the current design. If the 5% Stage 2 floor binds for most portfolios most of the time, the model output stops determining the reported provision. India then arrives at a standardised approach wearing ECL clothing: full modelling cost, no modelling benefit.

8  What is needed

Some standardisation of ECL parameters by product and ticket size.

The real problem is not comparability in the abstract — it is that most NBFCs simply do not have enough data to map Stage 2 and Stage 3 lifetime risk from their own book. A single lender sees too few migrations, censored at its observation window, and loses sight of the account entirely once it leaves the book.

Bureau data is the natural pooling layer for exactly this. It is market-wide, it observes DPD across all lenders, and it captures cure. Transition matrices and vintage curves built on it are a far more defensible anchor for lifetime PD than anything a mid-sized NBFC can construct alone, and they are transparent in a way an internal model is not.

Two limits should be stated honestly. Bureau data gives PD; it gives nothing on LGD or EAD, which are the two harder parameters. And India's bureau era contains no clean downturn — COVID was policy-distorted by moratorium and ECLGS — so pooling fixes small samples but does not create a stress observation that does not exist in anyone's data.

The better design is to standardise the benchmark, not the model. Publish a reference PD term structure by product and ticket size, calibrated on system-wide data. Lenders model freely but must disclose and justify deviation from the reference. Comparability then comes from the disclosed deviation rather than from a binding floor, and the deviation is what gets audited.

RBI's Stage 1 and Stage 2 minima are already standardisation — just imposed as a hard floor rather than as a benchmark, which is the version that makes the models redundant.

9  In my view

Opinion

This section is opinion. The preceding sections are not.

EIR. A step in the right direction, but it penalises growth portfolios and early-stage portfolios. Prepayment and default qualify the claim that it is neutral over the life of a loan. On balance the trade is reasonable — revenue gets recognised when it is truly earned.

ECL. Right in spirit, but going to the opposite extreme, without the amortising effect. The old regime rewarded lenders for booking income early and defaults late, and that had to be fixed. But the answer to a back-ended regime is not a fully front-loaded one — particularly when the standard already applies the amortising principle to credit-impaired assets and simply declines to generalise it.

Model dependence remains unresolved. Without standardisation by product and ticket size, ECL produces either numbers that are not comparable across lenders, or numbers set by floors, in which case the modelling apparatus is decoration. The industry is drifting toward the second without having chosen it.
